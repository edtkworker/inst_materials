01 · Structure

What the taxonomy organizes

A taxonomy groups related ideas so you can see a field’s structure and the relationships among its parts.

Chapter 10 brings together the theory, research, and practice discussed earlier in the book. It organizes instructional design (ID) into six domains, then links specific elements in each domain to their supporting theory bases.

The domains are categories, not a sequence of design steps. They can overlap and evolve as research, technology, and practice change.

How did the authors build it?

Some taxonomies derive categories from direct observation or statistical grouping. The authors built this one from their review of theory, research, and applications. Level 2 entries may be named concepts or actions a designer can take.

Which theories are brought together?

Chapters 2–9 cover general systems, communication, learning, early instructional, media, conditions-based, constructivist design, and performance improvement theory. A single element can draw on more than one.

How designers and researchers use the map

In research: Identify what to study

An element can point to a variable to study. The map can help researchers organize prior work, replicate a finding in a different setting, or identify a gap worth investigating.

Example: Feedback may be well studied in some settings. Do advisors learn to correct booking errors as well with automated feedback as with a coach’s feedback? The taxonomy helps frame that question. Data would be needed to answer it.

Using the taxonomy as a research guide, pp. 182–183

The authors’ closing point

The authors argue that theory supported by empirical research is the strongest basis for design decisions. Expertise also requires technical and procedural skill, and the field advances through careful inquiry and creative practice.
